# Theoretical Flaws vs. Practical Viability

Upon deeper critical analysis, the perceived "Advanced Flaws" at the edge of this compilation pipeline are actually highly manageable or naturally resolved by the hardware's architecture.

---

## 1. The "Virtual Fused Layer" Verification (Viability Check)
**The Initial Concern:** By tricking Timeloop with expanded loop bounds to model the "halo" of redundant computation, we might lose cycle-accurate micro-architectural fidelity. 

**The Viability Conclusion: Highly Viable.**
Timeloop calculates energy analytically by mapping the loop nest into aggregate MACs, SRAM reads/writes, and DRAM accesses. By mathematically expanding the $H$ and $W$ bounds of the virtual layer, Timeloop correctly registers the exact volume of redundant MACs and the exact increase in SRAM fetch requests. 
For standard spatial CNN accelerators, an expanded tile is processed by the hardware identically to a normal tile. While we might lose < 1% accuracy on negligible edge-case router stalls, as a macro-architecture energy predictor for the ILP, the Virtual Fused Layer is a perfectly viable and robust proxy.

## 2. Hardware FSM Rigidity and Segmented Buffers
**The Initial Concern:** The hardware Finite State Machine (FSM) might not be able to physically execute the complex schedule generated by the ILP without control divergence.

**The Viability Conclusion: The FSM Can Easily Handle It.**
This concern is completely neutralized by two specific realities of our pipeline and modern hardware:
1.  **Rectangular Tiling:** Because we explicitly constrained the ILP to only output "Rectangular Overlapped Tiling," the solver is mathematically forbidden from generating skewed or modulo loops. The output schedule is guaranteed to be a standard nested `for`-loop. Nested rectangular loops are the exact native language of hardware FSMs.
2.  **Segmented SRAM Buffers:** Our original math assumed a monolithic `GlobalBuffer` ($Vol_{out} + Vol_{in} + Vol_{weight} \le SRAM$). However, architectures like Eyeriss or Simba have strictly separated, dedicated SRAMs (e.g., Weight Buffer, Input Buffer, Output/Accumulation Buffer). 
    *   This makes the compilation *easier* and safer for the FSM! We don't have to worry about data colliding in a shared memory pool. We simply separate Stage 1 into three independent, simpler constraints: $Vol_{out} \le Buffer_{out}$, $Vol_{in} \le Buffer_{in}$, and $Vol_{weights} \le Buffer_{weights}$. 
    *   The FSM simply routes the tiles to their dedicated hardware ports exactly as instructed by the standard loop nest. The control logic remains trivial.
